Spiritual Healing

The Healing Dances of Sanni Yakuma

For centuries, traditional masks in Sri Lanka served as tools of spiritual healing and community connection. The most prominent ritual, the Sanni Yakuma, is an all-night exorcism ceremony designed to cure illnesses believed to be caused by Sanni demons.

There are 18 Sanni Demons (Daha Ata Sanni), each representing a specific physical or mental ailment—such as blindness (Kana Sanni), deafness (Kora Sanni), stomach distress (Amukku Sanni), or delirium. The chief exorcist and actors don hand-carved wooden masks depicting these demons, dancing before fires to appease, mock, and banish the afflictions back into the spirit world.

Complementing these solemn rituals is Kolam, a form of masked folk drama and comedy. Initiated in royal courts, Kolam plays depict caricatures of colonial officers, officers' wives, royal characters, and comedic village gossip (such as Nonchi and Jasaya) to entertain and teach moral lessons.

Hereditary Craft

From Swamp to Sacred Art

How a block of soft Kaduru wood is transformed into a spiritual guardian.

Step 01

Harvesting & Selection

Artisans search local marshlands for the Kaduru (Nux vomica) tree. Kaduru wood is specifically chosen because it is incredibly lightweight, easy to carve when fresh, and resists splitting. Its soft nature makes it comfortable for dancers to wear during night-long energetic performances.

Step 02

Geometric Proportions

Before carving begins, the artisan uses ancestral measurements recorded on ola-leaf manuscripts. The dimensions of fangs, bulging eyes, and flared nostrils must match ancient sacred equations to properly house the specific entity's aura.

Step 03

Manual Carving & Sculpting

Using a array of specialized hand chisels (Siyas), wooden mallets, and fine iron scrapers, the artisan sculpts the three-dimensional mask out of the cured block. Complex ornaments like cobra hoods (on Raksha masks) or wings are carved separately and jointed using local wooden dowels.

Step 04

Smoke Curing

The freshly carved mask is placed inside a smoking vault or hung high above the traditional hearth for up to three weeks. The smoke dries out residual moisture, cures the wood resins, and prevents future woodworms or rot, ensuring it lasts for generations.

Step 05

Natural Pigmentation

Finally, the mask is coated with yellow clay primer. Traditional painters prepare natural paints from mineral powders, crushed sea shells, lamp soot, and plant resins, painting detailed symbolic patterns with handmade bamboo-reed brushes.

Symbolic Guardians

The Raksha Demon hierarchy

Gurulu Raksha (Eagle)

Associated with the mythical bird Garuda (the mount of Lord Vishnu). This mask symbolizes total protection. Carved with stylized wings and holding a cobra in its beak, it represents the triumph over poison, evil intentions, and jealousy.

Mayura Raksha (Peacock)

The peacock is the mount of the war god Kataragama. Represented with fan-like peacock tails surrounding the ears, this mask symbolizes grace, wealth, and goodwill. It is hung in homes to draw positive friendships and tranquility.

Ginijal Raksha (Fire Devil)

Featuring standard demon fangs wrapped in expanding layers of fire sparks and tongues of flames. Ginijal represents raw fire energy. It is used to purify dirty spaces and conquer obstacles, evoking active motivation.

Maha Kola Raksha

The supreme demon king of healing. This massive, intricate mask depicts a central demon eating Sanni spirits, surrounded by 18 miniature Sanni masks. It represents control over all illnesses and the ultimate balance of life force.

Keeping Art Alive

The Legacy of Ambalangoda Artisans

Traditional mask-carving is a hereditary craft. For over two centuries, a few families in Ambalangoda, on the southern coast of Sri Lanka, have kept these traditions alive, transferring patterns, songs, and carving proportions from father to son (Garu Paramparawa).

In an era of plastics and mass production, **Ceylon Mask Ambalangoda** collaborates directly with these master families. By purchasing our masks, you are not just acquiring art; you are supporting sustainable local forestry of Kaduru wood, protecting traditional pigment recipes, and funding training programs for the next generation of carvers.
